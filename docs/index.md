<section class="wl-split wl-divider" aria-labelledby="question-title">
<div class="wl-reveal"><p class="wl-eyebrow"><span class="wl-section-number">01</span> The fundamental question</p><h2 id="question-title">Does the first move really matter?</h2><p class="wl-lede">Chess begins with an asymmetry. White moves first; Black responds. Across many games, does that single tempo correspond to a measurable difference in score?</p><p class="wl-lede">The rules are fixed, but the players are not randomly assigned to openings or opponents. These games let us measure a scoring association. They do not isolate the causal value of moving first.</p><a class="wl-text-link" href="research/question/">Understand the research question →</a></div>
<div class="wl-duality wl-reveal"><div class="wl-side wl-side-white"><span class="wl-piece" aria-hidden="true">♙</span><div><strong>White</strong><small>The first move.</small><p>Score = 1 for a win,<br>½ for a draw, 0 for a loss.</p></div></div><div class="wl-side wl-side-black"><span class="wl-piece" aria-hidden="true">♟</span><div><strong>Black</strong><small>The first response.</small><p>Different reply groups.<br>Different observed outcomes?</p></div></div></div>
</section>
<section class="wl-split wl-finding" aria-labelledby="h1-title">
<div class="wl-reveal"><p class="wl-eyebrow"><span class="wl-section-number">02</span> Finding one · H1</p><h2 id="h1-title">White scores above 50%. But by how much?</h2><p class="wl-lede">The primary weighted estimate is <strong>51.776%</strong>: an excess of <strong>1.776 percentage points</strong> over equal scoring. Its 95% confidence interval is <strong>51.653%–51.899%</strong>.</p><p class="wl-lede">That is expected score, not win probability. The corresponding weighted White win share is 49.533%; draws contribute half a point.</p><a class="wl-text-link" href="research/h1/">Read the H1 analysis →</a></div>

<section class="wl-divider" aria-labelledby="method-title"><p class="wl-eyebrow"><span class="wl-section-number">04</span> From games to evidence</p><h2 id="method-title">A question is only as strong as its method.</h2><ol class="wl-process"><li><h3>Collect the source games</h3><p>97,512,351 source games scanned from the March rated-standard Lichess dump.</p></li><li><h3>Validate and sample</h3><p>Deterministic ID sampling, recorded exclusions and verified accounting. 836,070 retained games.</p></li><li><h3>Weight the outcomes</h3><p>Inverse inclusion probabilities account for deliberately oversampled strata.</p></li><li><h3>Test the hypotheses</h3><p>10,000 simulations, bootstrap uncertainty and separate correction families.</p></li><li><h3>Challenge the result</h3><p>Rating adjustments, shared-player dependence, threshold and game-length sensitivities.</p></li></ol><a class="wl-text-link" href="methodology/data/">Follow the methodology →</a></section>
<section class="wl-caveats" aria-labelledby="caveats-title"><div class="wl-caveat-box"><p class="wl-eyebrow">Read the result with its boundaries</p><h2 id="caveats-title">Evidence has limits.</h2><ul class="wl-caveat-list"><li><strong>Observation, not randomization</strong>Color, player selection and opening choices are not a causal experiment.</li><li><strong>One month, one platform</strong>March 2025, selected Lichess rated controls. No full-year or over-the-board claim.</li><li><strong>Ratings are imperfect</strong>Rating adjustment cannot remove every difference between players.</li><li><strong>Players appear repeatedly</strong>Dependence matters. Dyadic sensitivities have their own assumptions.</li><li><strong>Subgroups have uncertainty</strong>Sparse reply groups, multiplicity and selected extremes constrain interpretation.</li><li><strong>Restrictions change the result</strong>The ≥40-ply sensitivity materially reduces H1's magnitude. Near-equal ratings weaken H2 evidence.</li></ul><a class="wl-text-link" href="research/limitations/">Read the limitations in full →</a></div></section>
